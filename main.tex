\abstract{\unboldmath Self-driving laboratories use multi-fidelity Bayesian optimization (MFBO) to combine abundant low-fidelity (LF) evaluations with scarce high-fidelity (HF) experiments. Gaussian processes (GPs) are standard but restrictive in molecular and materials spaces. We compare five transfer-learning (TL) architectures and three GP methods across thirteen tasks with matched budgets, fidelity schedules and initial designs. \mainclaim{GPs performed best on smooth Branin functions.} \mainclaim{In contrast, on every molecular and materials benchmark the base TL surrogate matched or exceeded the base multi-fidelity Gaussian process (MFGP).} \mainclaim{TL surrogates also required up to 22-fold fewer floating-point operations.} \mainclaim{On the three benchmarks where LF evaluations misrank the best candidates, MFGPs fell to the level of optimization using HF data alone, whereas base TL still performed better in final regret.} \mainclaim{Because the reliability of LF evaluations near the optimum is rarely known in advance, this robustness makes TL the more reliable surrogate for MFBO in molecular and materials design.}}

Molecular and materials discovery}

\section{Introduction}

\storyline{Self-driving labs close the loop; the surrogate sets discovery efficiency, and BO is the standard engine.}
Self-driving laboratories (SDLs) couple automated experimentation with algorithmic decision-making in a closed loop. Experiments are designed, executed and analysed autonomously. The resulting data update a surrogate model that guides the next experiments\citep{abolhasani2023rise,hickman2025atlas}. The surrogate and acquisition policy therefore determine the efficiency of discovery. In molecular and materials science, where evaluations are expensive, Bayesian optimization (BO) has become the dominant approach\citep{shahriari2015taking}.

\storyline{Single-fidelity BO trades cost against accuracy; MFBO combines cheap LF exploration with scarce HF confirmation.}
Conventional BO draws on a single source of information, which forces a trade-off between evaluation cost and accuracy. Low-fidelity (LF) evaluations such as approximate simulations are cheap but noisy or biased, whereas high-fidelity (HF) experiments are accurate but costly\citep{peherstorfer2018survey}. Multi-fidelity Bayesian optimization (MFBO) combines both, using inexpensive LF evaluations to explore the design space and scarce HF measurements to confirm the most promising candidates\citep{kennedy2000predicting,sabanza2025best}.

\storyline{GP surrogates dominate MFBO but scale poorly, and their shared-kernel coupling cannot correct a proxy that misleads only near the optimum.}
Most MFBO methods rely on Gaussian-process (GP) surrogates, whose strength is calibrated uncertainty. Multi-fidelity Gaussian processes (MFGPs) perform strongly in low-data regimes but scale poorly as LF data accumulate\citep{williams2006gaussian}. Their kernel-based similarity assumptions can also struggle with nonlinear, biased or location-dependent LF--HF relationships\citep{snoek2015scalable, seeger2004gaussian, perdikaris2017nonlinear}. Because the fidelities are coupled through one kernel shared across the design space, a proxy that misleads only near the optimum is corrected only as HF observations accumulate there. Such imperfect LF proxies are common in SDLs. They may be informative only in parts of the design space, misleading near the optimum or weakly correlated with the HF objective.

\storyline{TL scales linearly, transfers LF-learned representations, and lets HF data set the weight of the LF prediction.}
Transfer learning (TL) offers a way past these limits. A neural network trained on abundant LF data learns a representation of the design space at a cost that grows linearly with the data, and a Bayesian linear-regression (BLR) head on that representation yields predictive distributions at neural-network cost\citep{snoek2015scalable}. Features learned on one task transfer to related tasks with few labels\citep{yosinski2014transferable}, and in molecular property prediction, representations pretrained on LF data improve predictions from sparse HF measurements\citep{buterez2024transfer}. Because the head is fitted to the HF observations, the weight given to the LF prediction is set by the HF data themselves, so a proxy that is biased or informative only locally need not dominate the HF prediction. Composite networks can also learn nonlinear rather than kernel-prescribed relationships between fidelities\citep{meng2020composite}.

\storyline{Nobody has compared TL with MFGPs in a controlled closed loop; hypothesis: a more robust alternative to MFGPs for molecular and materials discovery.}
Yet this potential is untested. Such neural-network surrogates have driven closed-loop MFBO only in bespoke engineering-design settings\citep{li2020dnnmfbo}, whereas MFBO for molecules and materials has been built around GP surrogates\citep{sabanza2025best}. TL surrogates have not been compared with MFGPs under controlled closed-loop conditions. It is therefore unknown whether they outperform MFGPs, under which conditions, and through which mechanism, since any advantage could arise from better uncertainty, a better predictive mean or the representation of the design space. \mainclaim{We hypothesize that TL surrogates, which carry LF information into the HF prediction through a learned representation and an HF-fitted head, provide a more robust alternative to MFGPs for molecular and materials discovery.}

\storyline{Study design: compare discovery performance, isolate the conditions for an advantage, test its mechanism, and assess computational scaling.}
We test this hypothesis through a controlled comparison of five TL surrogates and three MFGPs on thirteen synthetic, molecular and materials tasks. First, we compare discovery performance under matched candidate representations, budgets, fidelity schedules and initial designs to establish where TL offers an advantage. Second, we examine when this advantage arises. Alongside the empirical benchmarks, a controlled fidelity-quality grid varies global LF--HF correlation and agreement among the best candidates independently, testing which property governs the relative performance of the two surrogate families. Third, we investigate what drives the advantage. Matched-acquisition comparisons and uncertainty-driven acquisition experiments assess the contribution of exploration, while feature and prediction-path ablations distinguish the roles of learned representations and the LF prediction carried into the HF model. HF-only controls further test whether using LF information improves discovery over spending the budget entirely on HF evaluations. Finally, we measure computational cost as observations accumulate to assess the scalability of repeated surrogate updates.

\storyline{Preview: GPs win on Branin; TL-base ties or beats GPs on every molecular/materials pool, most where the proxy misorders the optimum; mechanism is the transferred mean, plus cheaper compute.}
Together, these experiments show that the relative performance of TL and GP surrogates depends on the optimization setting. \mainclaim{GPs performed best on the smooth Branin functions, whereas our baseline TL surrogate (TL-base) matched or exceeded the autoregressive MFGP (GP-base) on every molecular and materials benchmark considered here. This pattern also held against the best-performing GP on eight of the nine molecular and materials benchmarks. Its advantage was greater where LF evaluations misrank the best candidates relative to the HF objective.} We find that this advantage is driven primarily by the transfer-learned predictive mean rather than uncertainty-driven exploration, and is more closely associated with LF--HF agreement near the optimum than with global LF--HF correlation. TL surrogates also showed more favourable computational scaling as observations accumulate over repeated optimization updates. These findings support TL-base as a more reliable surrogate choice than the GPs evaluated here for descriptor-based molecular and materials optimization, particularly when the reliability of LF evaluations near the optimum is unknown.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\storyline{How to read Fig.~1: attainment rewards reaching good candidates early and keeping them.}
We evaluated eight surrogates (Methods~\ref{sec:meth-surrogates} and~\ref{sec:meth-transfer}) on thirteen benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}) under a fixed optimization protocol (Methods~\ref{sec:meth-loop}). Fig.~\ref{fig:final_regret}b--n summarizes performance on each benchmark using attainment. This measures the fraction of the budget window spent at or below the pool's top 5, 2, 1 and 0.5\% regret thresholds. Values are averaged over the four targets and 40 seeds (Methods~\ref{sec:meth-impl}). A surrogate therefore scores highly only if it reaches good candidates early and keeps them. To compare performance across benchmarks, panels o and p show each surrogate's mean attainment deficit relative to the best-performing surrogate on each benchmark, averaged over all thirteen benchmarks and the nine molecular and materials benchmarks, respectively. Because this deficit measures the shortfall from the best attainment, lower values indicate better performance. Fig.~\ref{fig:regret_trajectory} shows the underlying regret trajectories.

\subsection{GP performance on smooth synthetic functions}\label{sec:res-branin}

\storyline{Synthetic functions: GPs lead on Branin, everything converges on Park; the GP edge is the smooth kernel prior.}
The GP family achieved the highest attainment on both Branin scenarios (Fig.~\ref{fig:final_regret}b,c). \readabilitymark{On Branin-Fav, GP-DKL and GP-base reached $0.91$ against $0.59$ for TL-base, and both led every TL variant. On Branin-Unfav, GP-base reached $0.76$ against $0.35$ for TL-base.} The two GP curves lie below every TL curve over almost the whole run (Fig.~\ref{fig:regret_trajectory}a,b). On the four-dimensional Park functions, most methods approached zero regret after a few HF evaluations (Fig.~\ref{fig:regret_trajectory}c,d). Attainment therefore separated them only by how early they converged. On Park-Fav, TL-base ($0.74$) trailed the best-performing GP ($0.85$), whereas TL-SPS and TL-PtJ exceeded it. On Park-Unfav, TL-base was $0.03$ below the best-performing GP (Fig.~\ref{fig:final_regret}d,e). \mainclaim{The GP advantage over TL-base is therefore largest on the smooth two-dimensional Branin functions, where the kernel prior matches the objective's structure.}

\subsection{TL performance on molecular and materials benchmarks}\label{sec:res-chem}

\storyline{Headline: TL-base $\ge$ GP-base on all nine pools and $\ge$ the best GP on eight; the five TL surrogates have the five smallest deficits.}
\mainclaim{Across the nine molecular and materials benchmarks, TL-base matched or exceeded GP-base in mean attainment on every pool.} \readabilitymark{Its margin was $0.02$--$0.16$ on seven pools. It also matched or exceeded the best-performing GP on eight pools, trailing by $0.03$ on Elastic-SevenNet (Supplementary Table~9).} Averaged over these nine benchmarks, the five TL surrogates had the five smallest mean deficits to the per-benchmark best. Their deficits spanned $0.01$--$0.06$, compared with $0.07$--$0.09$ for the three GPs (Fig.~\ref{fig:final_regret}f--n,p).

\storyline{Pool by pool: HOPV15 and the two band-gap pools separate the families; on the elastic pools both families track LF quality.}
The clearest advantages over the best-performing GPs occurred on HOPV15 and the two band-gap benchmarks. On organic photovoltaics (HOPV15), TL-base ($0.38$) exceeded GP-base ($0.32$), the best-performing GP on this pool (Fig.~\ref{fig:final_regret}i). On the two unsaturated band-gap benchmarks, Matbench-gap and ExptGap-PBE, the families' mean attainments separated completely, with the lowest TL attainment exceeding the highest GP attainment (Fig.~\ref{fig:final_regret}j,k). Although the families also separated on Polarizability, the difference there was confined to a range of $0.01$. On Matbench-gap, the TL surrogates also led the regret trajectories over the second half of the budget window (Fig.~\ref{fig:regret_trajectory}i). \readabilitymark{On the three elastic-modulus pools, TL-base exceeded GP-base by $0.12$--$0.13$. Against the best-performing GP, however, it stood within $0.02$ on two pools and $0.03$ below it on Elastic-SevenNet (Fig.~\ref{fig:final_regret}l--n).} These pools share one HF source but differ in candidate set and LF source (CHGNet, SevenNet and MatterSim, Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}). Both families attained more where the LF source agrees better with the HF values. This trend cannot be attributed to LF quality alone because the candidate sets also differ.

\storyline{Trajectories: the gap opens late, GPs plateau at the LF-favoured candidate while TL keeps improving (pick logs).}
On HOPV15 and Matbench-gap, the TL advantage emerged through continued improvement later in the optimization run (Fig.~\ref{fig:regret_trajectory}). On HOPV15, all surrogates started alike, but the GP family stopped improving after roughly 20 HF-equivalent units, whereas TL-base and the TL variants continued to improve (Fig.~\ref{fig:regret_trajectory}h). On Matbench-gap, the TL surrogates pulled ahead after roughly 15 units (Fig.~\ref{fig:regret_trajectory}i). The pick logs locate this plateau without explaining it. \readabilitymark{On HOPV15, GP-base and GP-SV ended at the candidate ranked first by the LF proxy in about half of the seeds. The TL surrogates kept improving after mid-budget in half to three quarters of the seeds, compared with a third to a half for GPs. They also reached the optimum about twice as often (Supplementary Table~11).} Promotion of LF-observed candidates did not separate the families, since every surrogate promoted nearly all its later HF picks.

\storyline{Transfer mechanism matters: no single surrogate dominates across the thirteen benchmarks.}
The TL advantage nevertheless depends on how transfer is implemented, with no single surrogate leading across all benchmarks. Averaged over the thirteen benchmarks, the eight surrogates lie within $0.07$ of one another in mean attainment. Their mean deficits to the per-benchmark best span $0.07$--$0.13$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:final_regret}o). GP-DKL has the smallest deficit because of its margin on the Branin functions, but no single mechanism dominates. Different surrogates are weakest on different pools. For example, TL-SPS and TL-PtJ fall below GP-base on HOPV15, whereas TL-base and TL-MMD lead the molecular and materials summary (Fig.~\ref{fig:final_regret}p). \mainclaim{Outcomes therefore depended on how transfer was implemented.}

\subsection{Dependence of the TL advantage on optimum agreement}\label{sec:res-grid}

\storyline{HF-only controls: TL-base remains competitive when LF evaluations misrank the best candidates.}
HF-only controls show that TL-base remains competitive when LF evaluations misrank the best candidates. This was observed on HOPV15, Matbench-gap and ExptGap-PBE. Here, the MFGPs attained no more than HF-only controls, except by a small margin on Matbench-gap (Supplementary Table~8). These controls used a single-fidelity GP (SFGP) or random HF sampling and started from the same initial points. TL-base, however, held the controls' level within $0.02$ on HOPV15 and ExptGap-PBE and exceeded it on Matbench-gap. On all three pools, it therefore exceeded the best-performing GP and ended at lower regret than GP-base.

\storyline{Contrast: TL-base and the best GP perform similarly where LF evaluations rank the HF optimum near the top; top-10 overlap is a retrospective diagnostic.}
By contrast, TL-base and the best-performing GP achieve similar attainment where LF evaluations rank the HF optimum near the top. On these pools (COFs, FreeSolv and Polarizability), TL-base and the best-performing GP differ by at most $0.01$ in attainment (Fig.~\ref{fig:final_regret}f--h). GP-base and every TL surrogate reach $0.90$--$0.96$, whereas the HF-only controls fall far behind, at $0.37$--$0.61$ (Supplementary Table~8). \readabilitymark{This pattern was also evident across the nine pools, where the margin between the best TL surrogate and best-performing GP followed the LF--HF top-10 overlap (Spearman $\rho = -0.88$). The three pools with zero overlap have the largest margins, whereas the six with higher overlap lie within $0.03$. For TL-base, the same three pools have the largest margins over the best-performing GP ($0.06$--$0.09$). The other six lie between $-0.03$ and $+0.02$.} \mainclaim{Because this retrospective association uses the HF ranking of the whole pool, it cannot serve as a diagnostic before HF data are collected.}

\storyline{Why the grid: on real pools $R^2$ and optimum agreement covary, so we vary them independently on Polarizability.}
On the empirical benchmarks, global LF informativeness and reliability near the optimum vary together. Either could therefore explain when TL wins. To separate them, we constructed a controlled grid of curated LF sources on the Polarizability substrate. The grid varies global LF--HF $R^2$ and top-10 optimum agreement independently, giving $126$ conditions with ten seeds each under the standard protocol (Methods~\ref{sec:meth-grid}). We compared three families pairwise on final regret, using the best member of each family in every condition. These were the TL surrogates, GP-base and the GP variants (Fig.~\ref{fig:family_heatmap}a--c).

\storyline{Grid result: TL never loses to GP-base; its advantage tracks agreement ($\rho \approx -0.8$), not $R^2$ ($\rho \approx 0$).}
\readabilitymark{The TL family was never worse than GP-base anywhere on the plane. It won in $81$ of $126$ conditions and tied in the remaining $45$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:family_heatmap}a). Against the GP variants, its advantage narrowed to $66$ conditions, with $60$ ties and no reversal (Fig.~\ref{fig:family_heatmap}b).} The GP representative therefore matters, because the variants themselves improved on GP-base in $81$ conditions (Fig.~\ref{fig:family_heatmap}c). The advantage is organized along the agreement axis rather than the $R^2$ axis. \readabilitymark{Its correlation with top-10 agreement was much stronger than with $R^2$ for the comparison with GP-base (Spearman $\rho = -0.81$ and $\rho = -0.09$, respectively). Against the GP variants, these correlations were $\rho = -0.78$ and $+0.11$ (lower row of Fig.~\ref{fig:family_heatmap}).} \mainclaim{Within this grid, the advantage therefore tracks preservation of the best candidates more strongly than global LF--HF correlation.}

\subsection{Source of the TL advantage}\label{sec:res-mechanism}\label{sec:res-acquisition}\label{sec:res-uq}

\storyline{Interpretation: the trajectories suggest a more useful TL predictive mean, but do not establish how LF information contributes.}
The trajectory differences on HOPV15 and Matbench-gap (Fig.~\ref{fig:regret_trajectory}h,i) are consistent with a TL predictive mean that better ranks promising candidates. A stationary kernel imposes one global notion of smoothness. In a descriptor space compressed by principal component analysis (PCA), however, the LF--HF discrepancy can vary strongly near the optimum. A GP can therefore misrank promising candidates. By contrast, the TL surrogates correct the LF prediction with a linear head that has few parameters to fit from sparse HF data. The improvement on HOPV15 occurs despite the LF signal having little global linear association with HF ($R^2 = 0.02$). However, the trajectories alone do not distinguish the contribution of representation expressivity from that of LF-to-HF transfer, nor do they exclude a contribution from uncertainty-driven exploration.

\storyline{Ablation: random features change little and HF-only networks lose; the advantage is the LF prediction acting as the additive baseline.}
\mainclaim{Feature and head-path ablations identify the LF prediction, rather than the learned representation, as the main source of the TL advantage.} On the nine molecular and materials pools, we tested this distinction by changing the inputs to the HF head (Supplementary Table~6). We replaced the head's learned representation with random features or reduced the head to the scalar LF prediction alone. With these modified heads, attainment stayed within $0.09$ on every pool for every surrogate, except the scalar head on Elastic-CHGNet. By comparison, a network of the same architecture and cost trained on HF data alone attained less than TL-base on every pool. Consistent with this interpretation, removing the LF prediction from the head lowered attainment on every pool, by $0.2$ or more on eight of the nine (Supplementary Table~7). \mainclaim{Further ablations show that the LF prediction contributes primarily as an additive baseline that the head corrects, whereas its contribution as an extra regression input is negligible.}

\storyline{Acquisition controls support the LF-informed mean as the main contributor to the TL advantage.}
We tested whether acquisition choice could explain the TL advantage (Methods~\ref{sec:meth-loop}). Comparing expected improvement (EI) and greedy selection for GP-base and TL-base showed no consistent improvement from switching either surrogate's default policy (Supplementary Fig.~1). Extending the comparison to five uncertainty-driven acquisitions across the TL surrogates likewise revealed no consistent gains over greedy selection (Supplementary Fig.~2). LF calibration also showed no consistent association with attainment (Supplementary Fig.~3), although this analysis does not assess the HF uncertainty used for acquisition. \mainclaim{Together with the head-path ablations, these results support the LF-informed predictive mean as the main contributor to the TL advantage within the evaluated surrogate construction.} Detailed analyses are provided in Supplementary Note~5.

\subsection{Surrogate benefits where LF screening fails}\label{sec:res-topk}

\storyline{Surrogate-free LF screen: two regimes; the need for a surrogate follows near-optimum preservation, not global correlation.}
We compared the optimization outcomes with a surrogate-free screen that ranks candidates by LF value and evaluates the top-ranked candidate at HF. Its screening regret indicates whether the LF proxy alone identifies a good candidate. It is not a budget-matched baseline (Fig.~\ref{fig:topk}a,b). The benchmarks split into two regimes at a screening regret of $0.3$ (Supplementary Note~7). In Regime~A, represented by Park-Fav and Polarizability, the top LF and HF candidates almost coincide. The screen therefore already identifies a near-optimal candidate. In Regime~B, represented by COFs and the Branin functions, high global correlation conceals poor agreement among the top-ranked candidates. For example, COFs has a Spearman rank correlation of $0.998$ but a screening regret of $4.52$. Nevertheless, every surrogate reached zero regret within the budget (Fig.~\ref{fig:regret_trajectory}e). HOPV15, Matbench-gap and the four larger materials pools also fall in Regime~B. The need for a surrogate therefore depends more on preservation of the near-optimal region than on global LF--HF correlation. On HOPV15 and Matbench-gap, the choice of surrogate matters as well (Fig.~\ref{fig:final_regret}i,j).

\subsection{Computational cost}\label{sec:res-cost}

\storyline{Compute: TL scales about linearly, GP-base cubically; TL is cheaper above $N \approx 46$ and up to 22-fold versus the GP variants.}
We quantified computational cost in floating-point operations (FLOPs) on nine benchmarks. Each profile covered one surrogate fit and one pool-wide prediction at five training-set sizes (Supplementary Note~8). Including the BLR head and residual network, TL surrogates scaled almost linearly with observation count (fit-FLOP exponent $k = 0.9$--$1.0$). GP-base scaled cubically, whereas GP-SV and GP-DKL had intermediate exponents but the largest constant factors (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling_law}). The family-level cost curves crossed at $N \approx 46$. Above this size, TL surrogates were cheaper to fit by a margin that grew with $N$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling_law}). GP-SV and GP-DKL were the most expensive surrogates on every profiled benchmark. \readabilitymark{Their refit and pool-wide prediction costs, integrated over the loop, reached roughly $22$-fold and $15$-fold those of the cheapest TL surrogate, respectively (Supplementary Note~8, Supplementary Fig.~4).} \mainclaim{Every TL surrogate cost fewer FLOPs than these two GP variants on every profiled benchmark. Savings relative to GP-base depended on pool size and budget, and GP-base was the cheapest surrogate on the smallest pools.} \mainclaim{Under the fixed protocol, TL-base combined attainment matching or exceeding GP-base on every molecular and materials benchmark with more favourable computational scaling.} This matters for SDL workflows, where abundant LF data and repeated updates make retraining a major component of closed-loop cost.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\storyline{Verdict: the hypothesis holds on the molecular and materials pools; TL-base trails the GPs on the synthetic functions; the advantage is robustness, at lower cost.}
An SDL must commit to a surrogate before HF data reveal whether its LF proxy is reliable near the optimum. We hypothesized that TL surrogates would be more robust to this uncertainty than MFGPs. \mainclaim{The comparison supports this hypothesis for descriptor-based molecular and materials pools.} TL-base trailed the best-performing GP on the synthetic functions, most on the smooth Branin functions. On the nine molecular and materials pools, it matched or exceeded GP-base everywhere and the best-performing GP on eight, trailing by $0.03$ on Elastic-SevenNet (Supplementary Table~9). The advantage was one of robustness. TL surrogates also cost fewer FLOPs than the two GP variants, by up to roughly $22$-fold (Supplementary Note~8).

\storyline{When: the families tie where the proxy preserves the optimum and separate where it misorders it; the grid isolates agreement, not $R^2$; correlation is the wrong axis, and agreement is unknowable in advance.}
Whether the advantage appears depends on how the LF proxy orders the best candidates. Where the proxy ranked the HF optimum near the top (COFs, FreeSolv and Polarizability), the families tied, far ahead of the HF-only controls. Where it misordered the optimum (HOPV15, Matbench-gap and ExptGap-PBE), the MFGPs fell to the level of those controls, whereas TL-base held or exceeded it (Supplementary Table~8). On real pools, correlation and agreement near the optimum vary together, but the controlled grid separated them: the TL advantage over GP-base tracked top-10 agreement (Spearman $\rho = -0.81$), not $R^2$ ($\rho = -0.09$), and no condition favoured GP-base. \mainclaim{Global correlation is therefore the wrong axis for choosing a surrogate, and also for deciding whether one is needed.} The surrogate-free LF screen failed on COFs despite a rank correlation of $0.998$ (Supplementary Note~7). A decision map indexed by global correlation alone, such as that of Sabanza-Gil et al.\citep{sabanza2025best}, thus needs an axis for agreement near the optimum, a quantity known only in retrospect. \mainclaim{A practitioner therefore needs a surrogate that stays competitive when agreement is unknown, the property TL-base displayed.}

\storyline{Why: the mechanism proposed in the Introduction, sharpened by the ablations: the LF prediction entering an HF-fitted head, not the representation and not exploration.}
The mechanism proposed in the Introduction explains why. An MFGP couples the fidelities through one kernel shared across the design space, so a proxy that misleads only near the optimum is corrected only as HF observations accumulate there. A TL surrogate instead fits its head to the HF observations, which set the weight of the LF prediction directly. \mainclaim{The ablations confirm this route but locate the advantage in the LF prediction entering the HF head, not in the learned representation.} Random features or a scalar LF-prediction head changed attainment little, whereas removing the LF prediction lowered it on every pool (Supplementary Tables~6 and~7). The LF prediction thus acts as an additive baseline that the head corrects with few parameters, which is why TL-base holds where the MFGPs fall: a few HF observations suffice to correct the baseline near the optimum, whereas a stationary kernel must relearn the local LF--HF discrepancy. Nor does the advantage come from exploration: neither swapping acquisition policies, nor five uncertainty-driven acquisitions for the TL surrogates, nor LF calibration changed the pattern (Supplementary Note~5). \mainclaim{Within this construction, the surrogate rather than the acquisition policy set performance, and the value of transfer lies in the predictive mean.}

\storyline{Limits: protocol scope, GP coverage and representative, tuning asymmetry, single-substrate grid, optimistic BLR uncertainty, retrospective pools.}
\mainclaim{These conclusions concern descriptor-based molecular and materials pools under a pool-based, two-fidelity protocol with a deterministic round-robin schedule.} Adaptive fidelity selection and hierarchical fidelities remain for future work. The three GPs do not span the range of GP methods, and the size of the advantage depends on the representative: against the stronger variants, the family-level gap on the grid narrowed by about two thirds, and on Park-Fav, Park-Unfav and Elastic-SevenNet TL-base exceeded GP-base yet trailed another GP. The TL hyperparameters are also fixed a priori whereas the GPs refit theirs at every iteration, which is conservative where TL wins but may inflate the Branin margin. \mainclaim{The mechanistic conclusions are likewise conditional.} The grid uses curated LF sources on a single substrate, so generalization rests on the benchmark suite. The BLR uncertainty is optimistic for TL-PtJ, TL-E2E and TL-SPS, whose representations are trained on the HF values that also fit the head (Supplementary Note~2), \mainclaim{so the limited value of exploration is specific to this construction.} The evaluation is retrospective on fixed candidate pools, so the surrogate ranking requires confirmation in deployment.

\storyline{Recommendation: TL-base by default where proxy reliability is unknown; GPs for smooth analytic objectives; improve the transferred mean first; no change to the acquisition policy.}
\mainclaim{Within these limits, we recommend TL-base as the default surrogate for descriptor-based molecular and materials pools whose LF proxy is of unknown reliability near the optimum.} It is specific to TL-base, because outcomes depended on the transfer mechanism: TL-SPS and TL-PtJ fell below GP-base on HOPV15. GPs remain preferable for smooth, low-dimensional analytic objectives such as Branin. \mainclaim{Surrogate development should first improve the transferred mean, with uncertainty-driven exploration a secondary concern.} Because only the surrogate changes, existing autonomous-experimentation platforms can adopt this guidance without altering their acquisition policy.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Problem setup}\label{sec:meth-setup}

\storyline{Formal setup: minimize $f^{(H)}$ over a compact space with a biased cheap $f^{(L)}$; global $R^2$, costs $(1, \rho)$, budget $B$, simple regret.}
MFBO uses inexpensive LF observations to guide the search for an expensive HF objective. We considered minimization under a finite experimental budget. The HF function $f^{(H)} : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ represents the target over a compact design space $\mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$. The aim is to identify its global minimizer, $x^\star = \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}} f^{(H)}(x)$. A cheaper LF function $f^{(L)}$ provides biased but potentially informative observations of the same landscape. We measured LF informativeness by its squared Pearson correlation with the HF objective, $R^2 = \mathrm{Corr}(f^{(L)}(x), f^{(H)}(x))^2$. This global LF--HF $R^2$ was evaluated over the candidate pool. For synthetic functions, we used $1{,}000$ uniform samples (Supplementary Note~4). Evaluation costs were $c_H = 1$ for HF and $c_L = \rho$ for LF, with $\rho \ll 1$. At each step, the optimizer selected a design point and fidelity pair $(x_t, s_t)$, where $s_t \in \{L, H\}$. The total budget constraint was $\sum_t c_{s_t} \le B$. Performance was measured by simple regret over HF evaluations, $r_T = \min_{t: s_t = H} f^{(H)}(x_t) - f^{(H)}(x^\star)$.

\subsection{Pool-based optimization loop and acquisition}\label{sec:meth-loop}

\storyline{Loop: fixed pool, farthest-point initial design, retraining from scratch, round-robin $\lfloor 1/\rho \rfloor$ LF per HF; GPs use EI for LF, TL is greedy; five-acquisition portfolio.}
Optimization was performed over a fixed candidate pool $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{cand}}$, with regret measured relative to its minimum HF objective value. A candidate already evaluated at one fidelity was excluded from further queries at that fidelity. Candidates screened at LF therefore remained available for HF evaluation. The initial design comprised two HF points and LF points of about the same total cost. The LF set contained 5 to 25 points, depending on $\rho$, giving 3 to 4.5 HF-equivalent units in all. We generated the design by farthest-point sampling on every pool. At each iteration, the surrogate model was retrained
de novo on all accumulated observations. A deterministic round-robin
schedule issued $n_{\mathrm{LF}\rightarrow\mathrm{HF}} = \lfloor 1/\rho \rfloor$
LF queries between successive HF queries. This isolates surrogate quality from cost-aware fidelity heuristics and reflects SDL pipelines with parallel LF and HF instruments. For the GP family, LF candidate selection used EI~\citep{jones1998efficient},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ei}
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_{\mathrm{EI}}(x) &= (\hat{y} - \mu(x) - \xi)\,\Phi(Z) + \sigma(x)\,\phi(Z), \\
Z &= \frac{\hat{y} - \mu(x) - \xi}{\sigma(x)},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{y}$ is the best observed HF value, $\xi = 0.01$~\citep{snoek2012practical}, and $\Phi$, $\phi$ denote the
standard normal cumulative distribution and probability density functions, respectively. The acquisition function was maximized by enumeration over the pool. For the
GPs, query locations at both fidelity levels were selected using the cross-fidelity posterior evaluated at the
HF level, which provides a predictive standard deviation in closed
form. The LF query maximized EI on this posterior with the HF incumbent. As for every surrogate, the HF query selected the lowest predicted HF mean among candidates not yet evaluated at HF. The main TL results used a greedy policy. At both fidelities, candidates were selected by the minimum predicted HF mean $\mu^{(H)}$ among the candidates not yet evaluated at that fidelity. To assess sensitivity to uncertainty modelling, we evaluated several uncertainty-driven acquisition policies.  We tested five acquisitions for the LF query, using the HF predictive mean, BLR standard deviation and HF incumbent. The portfolio comprised EI, probability of improvement~\citep{kushner1964new}, the GP lower confidence
bound~\citep{srinivas2012information}, max-value entropy search~\citep{wang2017max}, and
Thompson sampling~\citep{russo2018tutorial}, spanning the standard acquisition-function
taxonomy~\citep{shahriari2015taking}. The portfolio is analysed in
the Results (Supplementary Fig.~2) and reported in full in the Supplementary
Information. All objectives were formulated as minimization problems, with maximization
targets negated.

\subsection{Surrogate models}\label{sec:meth-surrogates}

\storyline{GP family implementations (GP-base, GP-DKL, GP-SV) and the two-network composite structure shared by the TL surrogates.}
Our GP family covered standard, learned-feature, and
sparse-variational regimes of MFGP modelling. GP-base was implemented using the
\texttt{SingleTaskMultiFidelityGP} in \texttt{BoTorch}~\citep{balandat2020botorch}, corresponding to an
autoregressive Kennedy--O'Hagan formulation~\citep{kennedy2000predicting} with a Mat\'{e}rn-5/2 kernel and fidelity
encoded as a binary indicator. \readabilitymark{Hyperparameters were fitted by exact
marginal-likelihood maximization with L-BFGS-B under the default priors of BoTorch 0.10.0, in double precision (Supplementary Note~1).} GP-DKL~\citep{wilson2016deep} uses a kernel defined on neural network features trained jointly by marginal-likelihood optimization. The network has widths 64, 64 and 16 with $\tanh$ activations. Its features feed a radial basis function (RBF) kernel with automatic relevance determination and a two-level index kernel. We optimized the model for 500 Adam steps in single precision. GP-SV approximated the multi-fidelity posterior by variational inference over up to 100 learned inducing points with a multi-task index kernel. We optimized it for 500 Adam steps on the full-batch evidence lower bound in single precision. This provides a scalable alternative to exact GP inference~\citep{titsias2009variational, hensman2013gaussian}. The TL surrogates use a composite two-network training structure related to multi-fidelity neural networks~\citep{meng2020composite}. An LF network $g_{\theta}^{(L)}:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}$ and an
HF residual network $g_{\phi}^{(H)}:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}$ (conditioned
on the input and the LF prediction) combine in the training loss as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:residual}
\text{}\mu^{(H)}_{\mathrm{aux}}(x) = g_{\theta}^{(L)}(x) + g_{\phi}^{(H)}\!\left([x,\, g_{\theta}^{(L)}(x)]\right).
\end{equation}

\storyline{HF loss is the squared error of the composite prediction; the residual network fits the LF--HF discrepancy on a shared $2 \times 64$ tanh backbone.}
For every transfer mechanism, the HF loss is the squared error of $\mu^{(H)}_{\mathrm{aux}}$ on HF observations. The residual network therefore fits the discrepancy $\delta(x) = f^{(H)}(x) - g_{\theta}^{(L)}(x)$. Where the mechanism lets this loss reach $\theta$, HF data also shape the LF representation. Both networks were two-layer multilayer perceptrons with hidden width 64 and $\tanh$ activations.

\storyline{BLR head: closed-form 66-coefficient read-out on the LF representation, giving a mean and variance from two HF points.}
The HF prediction comes from a BLR head on the LF network's final-layer representation. This adaptive-basis construction follows the principle of DNGO~\citep{snoek2015scalable,gawlikowski2023survey}, with the basis learned from abundant LF data. HF adaptation uses a closed-form linear read-out with 66 coefficients. It supplies a predictive mean and variance from as few as two HF observations. \readabilitymark{The HF prediction is $\mu^{(H)}(x) = g^{(L)}_{\theta}(x) + w^{\top}[\,g^{(L)}_{\theta}(x),\, \varphi^{(L)}(x),\, 1\,]$, where $\varphi^{(L)}$ is the LF network's final-layer representation.} The weights $w$ are fitted in closed form to HF residuals $y^{(H)} - g^{(L)}_{\theta}(x)$. Prior and noise precisions are fixed until four HF observations are available, then set by a leave-one-out rule (Supplementary Note~2). Every TL surrogate uses the same head and settings on every benchmark. The acquisition functions receive its predictive mean and standard deviation.

\storyline{What distinguishes the mechanisms: whether HF data reach the LF representation; in TL-base they do not.}
The residual network $g^{(H)}_{\phi}$ is trained as an auxiliary loss branch in every configuration. The transfer mechanisms differ in whether and how HF data reach the representation used by the head. TL-PtJ, TL-E2E and TL-SPS pass the HF loss to the LF network. TL-MMD passes only the alignment penalty, which sees HF inputs but not HF values. In TL-base, the LF prediction entering the residual network is detached. No HF gradient therefore reaches the LF network, and the linear head provides the entire HF adaptation (Supplementary Note~2). The Supplementary Information gives the derivation, shared architecture, training configurations and method-specific hyperparameters. Supplementary Table~1 lists each surrogate's fitting procedure, training length, learning rates, weight decay, scaling and predictive representation. It also specifies the kernel and likelihood or predictive head, inducing points and floating-point precision.

\subsection{TL framework}\label{sec:meth-transfer}

\storyline{The five TL surrogates by name; same backbone and head, differing only in how the LF representation is formed.}
The five TL surrogates comprise TL-base and four variants. These are pretrain-then-joint (TL-PtJ), end-to-end joint (TL-E2E), soft parameter sharing (TL-SPS) and domain adaptation with a maximum-mean-discrepancy penalty (TL-MMD). All share the two-network training structure of Eq.~(\ref{eq:residual}) and the BLR head described above. The LF network is trained on the larger LF dataset, alone or jointly with the residual network. The smaller HF dataset enters through the mechanism-specific loss and, at every refit, through the closed-form head fit. All configurations use the same backbone of two hidden layers with 64 $\tanh$ units. They differ only in how the LF representation is formed and whether HF data shape it.

\storyline{TL-base recipe: 300 epochs on the LF loss alone; HF data enter through the head only.}
\readabilitymark{TL-base trains the LF network for 300 epochs using Adam, learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$ and LF loss weight $0.5$.} The residual network trains concurrently on HF data, but the LF prediction it receives is detached. No HF gradient reaches the LF network, so LF data alone shape its representation. HF data enter the prediction only through the BLR head.

\storyline{TL-PtJ recipe: LF pretraining, then joint training with the HF loss reaching the LF network.}
TL-PtJ pretrains the LF network for 100 epochs at learning rate $10^{-3}$. Both networks then train for 100 further epochs at $10^{-4}$ on $0.3\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LF}} + 0.7\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{HF}}$. During this stage, the HF loss back-propagates into the LF network.

\storyline{TL-E2E recipe: joint training from scratch on both losses throughout.}
TL-E2E trains both networks from scratch for 300 epochs on $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LF}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{HF}}$. Learning rates are $10^{-3}$ for the LF network and $5\times10^{-4}$ for the HF network, with weight decay $10^{-4}$. The HF loss reaches the LF network throughout training.

\storyline{TL-SPS recipe: joint training with a first-layer weight-sharing penalty.}
\readabilitymark{TL-SPS~\citep{ruder2017overview} trains both networks from scratch for 200 epochs at $10^{-3}$ on $0.5\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LF}} + 0.5\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{HF}} + 0.01\,\|W^{(L)}_{1} - W^{(H)}_{1}\|^{2}$.} The penalty is the squared distance between the first-layer weights over the shared input columns. The HF loss again reaches the LF network.

\storyline{TL-MMD recipe: pretraining, then an MMD alignment penalty that sees HF inputs but not HF values.}
TL-MMD pretrains the LF network for 200 epochs at $10^{-3}$, then trains for 100 epochs at $10^{-4}$. This stage uses the HF task loss, with the LF prediction detached, plus $0.1$ times the maximum-mean-discrepancy~\citep{gretton2012kernel}. The discrepancy uses an RBF kernel of bandwidth 1 to compare LF-network features on LF data with HF-network features on HF data. Thus, the LF representation changes only through the alignment term and never through the HF loss.

\storyline{TL-base and TL-E2E differ only in whether the HF gradient reaches the representation; epoch and weight-decay settings are not separate methods.}
TL-base and TL-E2E share the LF training budget of 300 Adam epochs at $10^{-3}$ with weight decay $10^{-4}$. They therefore differ only in whether the HF gradient reaches the representation. Other LF schedules of 100 or 200 epochs, with or without weight decay, are settings of the same surrogate rather than separate methods. Weight decay ($10^{-4}$) is used by TL-base and TL-E2E and is absent from the other three configurations. Supplementary Note~3 gives full loss functions and method-specific hyperparameters.

The synthetic benchmarks were the two-dimensional Branin and four-dimensional Park functions~\citep{sabanza2025best}. We minimized them directly on lattices of $2{,}500$ and $256$ points, respectively, using the coordinates as model inputs. Each was evaluated under favourable and unfavourable fidelity relationships. A bias parameter $\alpha \in [0,1]$ controlled the LF--HF discrepancy, with $\alpha = 1$ giving identical fidelities. Supplementary Note~4 and Supplementary Table~5 give the function definitions and scenario settings.

\storyline{COFs: Xe/Kr selectivity, 14 raw descriptors, GCMC HF against Henry's-law LF.}
The nine molecular and materials benchmarks were empirical datasets. COFs contained $608$ covalent organic frameworks, with dimensionless Xe/Kr selectivity as the maximization objective. Its $14$ structural and compositional descriptors were used directly as model inputs. Grand-canonical Monte Carlo simulations supplied HF values, and Henry's-law estimates supplied LF values ($\rho = 0.065$, as in refs.~\citep{gantzler2023multifidelity,sabanza2025best}).

\storyline{FreeSolv: maximize hydration free energy (opposite direction to Sabanza-Gil et al.), experimental HF against computed LF.}
FreeSolv contained $640$ molecules with experimental (HF) and computed (LF) hydration free energies in kcal\,mol$^{-1}$~\citep{mobley2014freesolv}. We sought the largest, or most positive, hydration free energy, which identifies the pool's least hydrophilic molecule. The optimum is $+3.43$~kcal\,mol$^{-1}$ for octafluorocyclobutane, and the optimizer minimizes $y = -\Delta G_{\mathrm{hyd}}$. This direction is opposite to ref.~\citep{sabanza2025best}, which seeks the most negative hydration free energy. The cost ratio $\rho = 0.1$ follows that study.

\storyline{Polarizability: experimental HF against Hartree--Fock LF in normalized units.}
The Polarizability benchmark contained $1{,}134$ molecules from the Alexandria library, with experimental HF values and Hartree--Fock LF values~\citep{ghahremanpour2018alexandria, fare2022multi}. The objective is to maximize polarizability. Both fidelities are min--max normalized to $[0, 1]$ in the released pool. The objective and all regrets therefore use normalized units ($\rho = 0.167$, as in refs.~\citep{fare2022multi,sabanza2025best}).

\storyline{HOPV15: experimental PCE against a Scharber-model proxy from measured HOMO and gap; $\rho = 0.1$ is an assumption.}
HOPV15 contained $240$ donor molecules from the Harvard Organic Photovoltaic dataset~\citep{lopez2016harvard}. The HF objective is experimental power conversion efficiency (PCE, \%), to be maximized. The LF proxy is Scharber-model PCE~\citep{scharber2006design}, evaluated from each donor's experimentally reported HOMO level and optical gap. \readabilitymark{It uses $V_{\mathrm{OC}} = -E_{\mathrm{HOMO}} - E^{\mathrm{acc}}_{\mathrm{LUMO}} - \Delta$, with PC$_{61}$BM acceptor level $E^{\mathrm{acc}}_{\mathrm{LUMO}} = 4.3$~eV and empirical loss $\Delta = 0.3$~V. Here, $J_{\mathrm{SC}}$ is the AM1.5G photocurrent above the gap at an external quantum efficiency of $0.65$. The fill factor is $0.65$, and incident power is $100$~mW\,cm$^{-2}$.} The electrochemical gap replaced a missing optical gap for $46$ of the $240$ molecules. The LUMO--HOMO difference was the final fallback, although no molecule required it. We dropped $110$ entries from the $350$-molecule experimental set because they lacked a HOMO level, gap or PCE. The proxy is a closed-form estimate from measured quantities rather than a calculation. Its cost ratio $\rho = 0.1$ is therefore an assumption of this work, chosen to match FreeSolv.

\storyline{Matbench-gap: $|E_g - 1.4|$ target transformation, 66\% metals, $\rho = 0.05$ chosen to give 20 LF per HF.}
Matbench-gap contained $3{,}347$ inorganic compositions with experimental HF and PBE-computed LF band gaps in eV~\citep{dunn2020benchmarking}. The objective is the gap closest to the photovoltaic target of $1.4$~eV. We therefore replaced both fidelities by $|E_g - 1.4~\mathrm{eV}|$ before optimization. Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks} reports $R^2$ for these transformed values. Metals make up $66\%$ of the pool, with $E_g = 0$, and all map to $1.4$~eV. PBE gaps were averaged over polymorphs sharing a reduced formula. We assumed a cost ratio of $\rho = 0.05$. A PBE calculation is far cheaper than a synthesis-and-measurement cycle, but a literal ratio would imply hundreds of LF queries per HF query. The chosen value instead gives $20$ under the round-robin schedule.

\storyline{ExptGap-PBE: experimental against PBE gaps, same target transformation, 70\% metals.}
Four larger materials pools complete the suite. ExptGap-PBE pairs experimental gaps for $3{,}508$ inorganic compositions~\citep{zhuo2018predicting} with PBE gaps from the Materials Project~\citep{jain2013commentary}. Both sources came from matminer~\citep{ward2018matminer}. Entries sharing a reduced formula were averaged and matched by formula. The pool uses the same $1.4$~eV target and transformation as Matbench-gap, and $70\%$ of its entries are metals.

\storyline{Elastic pools: one DFT HF source, three universal-potential LF sources with differing candidate sets, so not a controlled LF comparison.}
The three elastic-modulus pools share one HF source. This is the DFT Voigt--Reuss--Hill shear modulus for Materials Project compounds in the Matbench \texttt{log\_gvrh} task~\citep{dunn2020benchmarking,dejong2015charting}. The modulus is maximized in GPa, so $y = -G_{\mathrm{VRH}}$. Each pool pairs this source with LF moduli recomputed through the matcalc elasticity workflow~\citep{matcalc}. Structures were first relaxed using one of three pretrained universal machine-learned interatomic potentials. These were CHGNet~\citep{deng2023chgnet}, SevenNet~\citep{park2024scalable} and MatterSim~\citep{yang2024mattersim}. \readabilitymark{Their LF--HF $R^2$ values against DFT increase in that order ($0.29$, $0.41$ and $0.80$). The pools contain $10{,}819$, $10{,}460$ and $10{,}946$ compounds for which the respective potential returned a finite, positive modulus. SevenNet calculations covered $10{,}560$ of the $10{,}987$ structures. The candidate sets therefore differ, as do their maximum HF shear moduli ($523$, $383$ and $523$~GPa).} Descriptor PCA was also fitted per pool. These are related benchmarks in which candidate set and LF source vary together, rather than a controlled comparison of LF sources alone.

\storyline{Larger pools: Magpie descriptors to PCA-10, $\rho = 0.05$ by schedule convention rather than cost, $B = 30$.}
All four larger pools use composition-based Magpie descriptors~\citep{ward2016general} reduced to ten dimensions by PCA, with $\rho = 0.05$. For the elastic-modulus pools, this ratio is an assumption using the same schedule convention as the band-gap pools. It is not the ratio of calculation costs, which is of order $10^{-3}$. The evaluation budget is $B = 30$, as on every molecular and materials pool.

\storyline{Descriptors: RDKit (210) and Magpie (132) reduced to PCA-10 once per pool; $\rho$ only sets query counts.}
The three molecular pools use RDKit 2D descriptors ($210$), and the five inorganic pools use composition-based Magpie descriptors ($132$). We standardized these descriptors and reduced them to ten dimensions by PCA fitted once on the whole pool at initialization (Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}, $d_{\mathrm{in}}$). The $14$ COF descriptors and synthetic lattice coordinates were standardized and used directly. Cost ratios for the synthetic functions, COFs, FreeSolv and Polarizability follow the studies introducing these pools~\citep{sabanza2025best,gantzler2023multifidelity,fare2022multi}. This permits indirect comparison with their SFGP and MFGP results. Ratios for HOPV15 and the five inorganic pools were set in this work as described above (Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}, $\dagger$). In every case, cost enters only through query accounting and the round-robin schedule. Thus, $\rho$ sets the number of LF queries per HF query and the LF share of the initial design, rather than a measured cost.

\subsection{Controlled fidelity-quality grid}\label{sec:meth-grid}

\storyline{Grid construction: random-forest LF sources with a bulk perturbation ($R^2$) and a gentle-demotion perturbation (agreement).}
We constructed a grid of curated LF sources on the Polarizability substrate to vary two properties of LF quality independently. Each source started from out-of-fold random-forest predictions of the HF objective. A bulk perturbation degraded global LF--HF $R^2$. A separate gentle-demotion perturbation selectively lowered top-ranked candidates while leaving most of the ranking intact. This reduced top-10 optimum agreement, defined as the overlap $|\text{LF top-10} \cap \text{HF top-10}|/10$. The top-$k$ overlap in Fig.~\ref{fig:topk}a uses the same definition, with $k$ expressed as a fraction of the pool. We used random-forest predictions because their errors carry the smooth, descriptor-correlated structure of a realistic LF proxy. Directly perturbing ground-truth values would instead introduce arbitrary, unstructured noise. These synthetic degradations need not reproduce real experimental or simulation biases. This limits generalization beyond the Polarizability substrate.

\storyline{Grid protocol: 126 conditions, ten seeds, $B = 50$, $\rho = 0.1$, reduced initial design.}
\readabilitymark{Calibrating the two perturbations produced $126$ conditions covering $R^2 \approx 0.1$--$0.9$ and agreement $0$--$1$. Each condition ran for ten seeds under the pool-based loop of Methods~\ref{sec:meth-loop}, with seeds shared across surrogates. The budget was $B = 50$ HF-equivalent units at cost ratio $\rho = 0.1$. Each run included $25$ HF and $249$ LF evaluations. The reduced initial design used two HF and five LF points, accounting for $5\%$ of the budget.}

\storyline{Grid scoring: best-of-family per cell, advantage sign convention, final regret and anytime area, Spearman $\rho$ against each axis.}
We compared three families pairwise. The TL family included all five surrogates, each run on every condition ($126$ conditions $\times$ five surrogates $\times$ ten seeds). GP-base formed the second family, and GP-DKL and GP-SV formed the third. Family performance in each cell was the lowest seed-mean metric among its members (best-of-family). Advantage was the second-named family's metric minus the first-named family's metric. Both metrics favour lower values, so positive advantage favours the first-named family. We scored final regret at the budget and anytime performance. The latter is the area under the simple-regret-versus-budget curve and rewards fast convergence rather than only the endpoint. Maps show the mean advantage in bins of ($R^2$, agreement), using one symmetric colour scale across comparisons. Counts above each map give the conditions favouring the first-named family and the exact ties. We summarized axis dependence using the Spearman rank correlation $\rho$ between $126$ per-cell advantages and either $R^2$ or top-10 agreement. Marginal profiles show advantage against agreement averaged over $R^2$, and against $R^2$ averaged over agreement, as mean $\pm$ s.e.

\subsection{Feature ablation and HF-only controls}\label{sec:meth-ablation}

\storyline{Ablations and HF-only controls: random features, scalar-LF head, LF-prediction removal, HF-only network, SFGP and random sampling, with pick logs.}
We ran feature and head-path ablations and HF-only controls on the nine molecular and materials pools under the main protocol. They used 40 seeds, $B = 30$, and the same initial designs, fidelity schedule and greedy policy. The ablations changed only the BLR head's input. We replaced the learned representation $\varphi^{(L)}$ with second-hidden-layer features from an untrained network of the same architecture. We also reduced the head to the scalar LF prediction. Separate ablations removed the LF prediction as an additive offset, a regression input or both. An HF-only network of the same architecture and training length was fitted to HF observations alone, with the same head. The HF-only controls started from the same two initial HF points and spent the whole budget on HF evaluations. These were SFGP with EI and uniformly random HF sampling. SFGP used BoTorch \texttt{SingleTaskGP} with a Mat\'{e}rn-5/2 kernel and library defaults. For each HF query, pick logs recorded the candidate, its LF-proxy rank and whether it had already been evaluated at LF. Supplementary Note~6 gives definitions and values.

\subsection{Implementation and reproducibility}\label{sec:meth-impl}

\storyline{Training details: full-batch Adam with fixed epochs and a priori hyperparameters; GPs off the shelf; every model retrained from scratch.}
We trained the five TL surrogates with full-batch Adam on mean squared error, using fixed epoch budgets. TL-base and TL-E2E include $\ell_2$ weight decay ($10^{-4}$), whereas the other three configurations do not. No dropout, batch normalization or gradient clipping was applied. TL hyperparameters were set a priori to common defaults, without benchmark-specific tuning (Supplementary Tables~2 and~3). The three GPs used their libraries' off-the-shelf configurations. GP-base maximizes the exact marginal likelihood using L-BFGS-B under the default priors of BoTorch 0.10.0, in double precision. GP-DKL uses 500 Adam steps on the exact marginal likelihood. GP-SV uses 500 Adam steps on the full-batch evidence lower bound, with up to 100 learned inducing points. Both GP variants use single precision without priors or weight decay. We retrained every model from scratch at each optimization iteration to remove path dependence from warm-starting.

\storyline{Standardization, and what is fixed across all eight surrogates versus what differs across families.}
Every TL surrogate standardizes inputs and targets at each refit using statistics pooled across fidelities. GP-base standardizes pooled targets through the \texttt{Standardize} outcome transform and takes inputs as supplied. GP-DKL and GP-SV take both inputs and targets as supplied. Descriptor standardization, PCA and standardization of the resulting features were applied once to the fixed pool. Every surrogate therefore receives the same standardized features, comprising ten PCA scores, 14 COF descriptors or synthetic lattice coordinates. Across all eight surrogates, we fixed the candidate pool and representation, budget, cost ratio, fidelity schedule, initial design, retraining schedule and seeds. The five TL surrogates also share a backbone of two hidden layers with 64 $\tanh$ units, optimizer, scaling and predictive head. Only the transfer mechanism varies within this family. Model capacity is matched within the TL family. Across families, fitting procedures, training lengths, scaling and precision differ as documented in Supplementary Table~1.

\storyline{Seeds 42--81 (GP-SV 36 and 39 on Branin), mean $\pm$ s.e.; 0.01 counts as matching, 0.02--0.03 as a small margin.}
Each benchmark-surrogate pair was evaluated for 40 random seeds (42--81). GP-SV on Branin-Fav and Branin-Unfav had 36 and 39 successful runs, respectively. The grid, acquisition controls and compute profiles used the seed sets specified in their descriptions. Reported values are means over seeds with the standard error of the mean. Mean attainments differing by $0.01$ or less are described as matching. Differences of $0.02$--$0.03$ are reported as small margins.

\storyline{Attainment definition, and the Fig.~1o,p deficit summary.}
Attainment is computed per seed from the best-so-far regret trajectory (Fig.~\ref{fig:final_regret}). For each of four targets, it measures the fraction of the window spent at or below that target. The window runs from the end of the initial design to budget $B$. Targets are the regrets of candidates at the top 5, 2, 1 and 0.5\% of the pool's HF ranking. We average over the four targets, assigning 0 to a target never reached. \readabilitymark{The summary in Fig.~\ref{fig:final_regret}o,p subtracts each surrogate's mean attainment from the highest mean attainment within each benchmark, then averages over benchmarks.} Differences between surrogates in this summary equal differences in mean attainment. Standard errors are taken over the per-benchmark values. Mean attainment gives the same ordering (Supplementary Table~10).

\storyline{Software environment, two run environments, TL runs bit-identical, GP-SV not always.}
The reference environment uses Python 3.9, PyTorch 2.4.1, BoTorch 0.10.0, GPyTorch 1.11 and SciPy 1.13.1. Versions are pinned in the code release's \texttt{requirements.txt}. Runs used these library versions in two environments. The SLURM cluster used Python 3.9 with NumPy 1.26.4, and the virtual machine used Python 3.12 with NumPy 2.0.2. The 840 TL runs executed in both environments gave identical trajectories. Repeated GP-SV fits with the same seed were not bit-identical in 19 of 120 repeated seeds on FreeSolv, HOPV15 and Matbench-gap. The Supplementary Information provides common and model-specific hyperparameters, GP configurations, the BLR derivation, synthetic function definitions and acquisition variants.
