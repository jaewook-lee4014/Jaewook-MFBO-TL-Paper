\suppnote{Surrogate configurations}{suppnote:mfgp}

Supplementary Table~\ref{supptab:surrogate_config} documents the configuration of each of the eight surrogates. It lists fitting procedures, training lengths, learning rates, weight decay, scaling, predictive representations, kernels and likelihoods or heads, inducing points and floating-point precision. Across all eight, we fixed the candidate pool and standardized features (Methods), budget, cost ratio, fidelity schedule, initial design and seeds. Initial designs used farthest-point sampling from the same seed, and every surrogate was retrained from scratch at each refit. The five transfer-learning surrogates also share a backbone of two hidden layers with 64 $\tanh$ units. They use full-batch Adam with fixed epoch budgets and no early stopping, pooled standardization of inputs and targets, and the same predictive head. The three GPs use off-the-shelf configurations from BoTorch 0.10.0 and GPyTorch 1.11. Their fitting procedures, training lengths, scaling and precision differ both from transfer learning and from one another, as documented in the table.

\subsection{GP-base}

GP-base is the autoregressive multi-fidelity GP (MFGP)~\citep{kennedy2000predicting}, implemented as BoTorch 0.10.0's \texttt{SingleTaskMultiFidelityGP}~\citep{balandat2020botorch}. It uses a Mat\'{e}rn 5/2 kernel with automatic relevance determination (ARD) over input dimensions and a linear truncated kernel over fidelity. We used the library's default priors. The unbiased and biased Mat\'{e}rn lengthscales have Gamma(3, 6) and Gamma(6, 2) priors, respectively. The fidelity power has a Gamma(3, 3) prior, and the output scale has Gamma(2, 0.15). The noise variance has a Gamma(1.1, 0.05) prior and a lower bound of $10^{-4}$. The mean is a learned constant. Inputs enter as supplied, with fidelity appended as a 0/1 column and no input transform. Targets from both fidelities are standardized together through the \texttt{Standardize} outcome transform.

At each iteration, we retrained the GP from scratch by maximizing the exact marginal log-likelihood under these priors (the posterior density). Fitting used L-BFGS-B through \texttt{fit\_gpytorch\_mll} in double precision. Predictions came from the posterior at the high-fidelity (HF) level $s=1$, whose standard deviation describes the latent function. Acquisition at both fidelities uses this single cross-fidelity posterior. EI with the HF incumbent selects the LF query, and the lowest posterior mean selects the HF query (Methods).

\subsection{GP-DKL}

GP-DKL, the deep-kernel GP~\citep{wilson2016deep}, is a GPyTorch exact GP with a kernel acting on learned features. Its feature network has layer widths 64, 64 and 16, with $\tanh$ hidden activations. Outputs are rescaled to $[-1, 1]$ by \texttt{ScaleToBounds}. The covariance multiplies an RBF kernel with ARD over the 16 features by a two-level, rank-1 index kernel carrying fidelity. It uses a constant mean and Gaussian likelihood, without priors. The feature network, kernel, mean, index kernel and likelihood are optimized jointly on the exact marginal likelihood. Training uses 500 full-batch Adam steps, with learning rate $10^{-3}$ for the network and $10^{-2}$ for GP parameters. Computation uses single precision without weight decay. Inputs and targets enter as supplied, and the predictive distribution includes learned observation noise.

\subsection{GP-SV}

GP-SV, the sparse variational MFGP~\citep{titsias2009variational,hensman2013gaussian}, is a GPyTorch approximate GP with a Cholesky variational distribution. It uses $\min(100, N)$ inducing points, initialized as a random subset of the $N$ training inputs from both fidelities. Inducing locations are optimized with the hyperparameters. The covariance multiplies an RBF kernel with ARD over the $d$ inputs by the same two-level index kernel. The model uses a constant mean and Gaussian likelihood, without priors. All parameters are optimized on the variational evidence lower bound over the full training set, without mini-batching. Training uses 500 Adam steps at learning rate $10^{-2}$ in single precision, without weight decay. The inducing-point count equals the training-set size until it reaches 100 and stays at 100 thereafter. Inputs and targets enter as supplied, and the predictive distribution includes learned observation noise. Repeated fits with the same seed are not bit-identical (Methods).


\suppnote{Uncertainty quantification via Bayesian linear regression}{suppnote:blr}
The transfer-learning surrogates use Bayesian linear regression (BLR) heads on the low-fidelity (LF) network's last-layer features. These heads provide the HF prediction and uncertainty, and the LF predictive distribution. The adaptive-basis construction follows the principle of DNGO~\citep{snoek2015scalable}. Here, however, the basis is learned from abundant LF data rather than target data. The residual network in Methods Eq.~(\ref{eq:residual}) enters the training loss only. Acquisition functions use the closed-form head's predictive mean and variance. Let $\varphi(x) \in \mathbb{R}^D$ denote the penultimate-layer representation and $\tilde{\varphi}(x) = [\varphi(x), 1]$ the augmented features. The model has a Gaussian prior over weights $w$,
\begin{equation}
y \mid x \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\tilde{\varphi}(x)^\top w,\, \beta^{-1}\right), \qquad w \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\, \alpha^{-1} I\right),
\end{equation}
with prior precision $\alpha$ and noise precision $\beta$. The closed-form posterior
yields predictive moments
\begin{equation}
\mu(x) = \tilde{\varphi}(x)^\top m_N, \qquad \sigma^2(x) = \beta^{-1} + \tilde{\varphi}(x)^\top S_N\, \tilde{\varphi}(x),
\end{equation}
where $(m_N, S_N)$ are the posterior mean and covariance. We use two such heads. The LF head has $\alpha = \beta = 1$ and supplies the predictive distribution used for calibration in Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}. The HF head regresses residuals $y^{(H)} - g^{(L)}_{\theta}(x)$ on $[\,g^{(L)}_{\theta}(x),\, \varphi^{(L)}(x),\, 1\,]$ to make the HF prediction (Methods). With at least four HF observations, leave-one-out error selects the representation block's prior-to-noise precision ratio from $\{10, 30, 100, 300, 1000\}$. The ratios for the $g^{(L)}_{\theta}$ coefficient and bias are $10$ and $1$, respectively. Noise precision is the inverse leave-one-out mean squared error, clipped to $[1, 100]$. With fewer than four HF observations, selection is skipped. The representation block then retains prior precision 100 with $\beta = 10$.

The primary greedy policy queries both fidelities by the HF predictive mean $\mu^{(H)}$. The five-acquisition portfolio instead selects LF queries using this head's HF predictive mean, standard deviation and HF incumbent (Supplementary Note~\ref{suppnote:acquisition_variants}). The head treats the representation as a fixed basis. In TL-PtJ, TL-E2E and TL-SPS, however, the HF loss also reaches the LF network. Their representations have therefore already been trained on the HF values used to fit the head. Neither leave-one-out selection nor the head's posterior covariance accounts for this earlier fit. Their HF predictive standard deviations are consequently optimistic relative to configurations whose representations never receive HF values. In TL-MMD, the alignment penalty passes HF inputs, but not HF values, to the LF network. This limitation concerns only the acquisition portfolio of Supplementary Note~\ref{suppnote:acquisition_variants}. The primary greedy policy uses the HF mean alone, and the calibration analysis uses the LF head.

\subsection{Training procedures and method-specific parameters}
\label{suppsec:training_procedures}
TL-base and TL-E2E use weight decay ($\ell_2$, 1e-4). The other three configurations use none.

\emph{TL-base.} Both networks train from scratch for 300 joint epochs on $0.5\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{LF}} + 0.5\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{HF}}$. Training uses Adam with learning rate 1e-3 and weight decay 1e-4. The LF prediction entering the residual network is detached, so no HF gradient reaches the LF network. Thus, LF data alone shape the representation, and HF data enter the prediction only through the BLR head (Supplementary Note~\ref{suppnote:blr}). This is the \texttt{DNGOJoint} configuration in the code release. Its residual network enters the training loss only.

\emph{TL-PtJ.} Stage 1 pretrains on LF data. Stage 2 trains jointly on both fidelities at a reduced learning rate, using $0.3\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{LF}} + 0.7\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{HF}}$.

\emph{TL-E2E.} Joint training uses full gradient flow and loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{LF}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{HF}}$. Learning rates are 1e-3 for the LF network and 5e-4 for the HF network.

\emph{TL-SPS.} Joint training uses $0.5\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{LF}} + 0.5\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{HF}} + \lambda_{\text{soft}}\,\Omega$. The penalty is $\Omega = \bigl\|W^{(L)}_{1} - W^{(H)}_{1,\,\cdot\,,\,1:d}\bigr\|_F^2$, the squared Frobenius distance between first-layer weight matrices over the $d$ shared input columns. The HF-network column receiving the LF prediction is not penalized. Neither are the biases, second layers or output layers. The penalty weight is $\lambda_{\text{soft}}=0.01$.

\emph{TL-MMD.} A maximum-mean-discrepancy penalty with weight $\lambda_{\text{MMD}}=0.1$ aligns LF and HF features. An RBF kernel of bandwidth 1 compares LF-network hidden features on LF training inputs with HF-network features on HF training inputs. The LF set is truncated to its first $n_{\text{HF}}$ points. Both networks update at learning rate 1e-4 during the HF stage. This stage excludes the LF loss, so the LF network changes only through the alignment penalty.

All residual networks enter the training loss only under the BLR head of Supplementary Note~\ref{suppnote:blr}. Prediction therefore differs according to whether HF data reach the LF representation. In TL-base, detaching the LF prediction before the residual network leaves the representation shaped by LF data alone. TL-PtJ, TL-E2E and TL-SPS update it through the HF loss. TL-MMD updates it only through the alignment penalty, which sees HF inputs but not HF values. Supplementary Table~\ref{supptab:surrogate_config} summarizes these settings for all eight surrogates.

\paragraph{Scharber proxy for HOPV15.}
The LF value of each donor is the Scharber-model power conversion efficiency~\citep{scharber2006design},
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
V_{\mathrm{OC}} &= \max\{0,\; -E_{\mathrm{HOMO}} - E^{\mathrm{acc}}_{\mathrm{LUMO}} - \Delta\}, &\quad E^{\mathrm{acc}}_{\mathrm{LUMO}} &= 4.3~\mathrm{eV}~(\mathrm{PC}_{61}\mathrm{BM}),\; \Delta = 0.3~\mathrm{V},\\
J_{\mathrm{SC}} &= \mathrm{EQE}\cdot J_{\max}(E_g), &\quad \mathrm{EQE} &= 0.65,\\
\mathrm{PCE}\,(\%) &= 100\, V_{\mathrm{OC}}\, J_{\mathrm{SC}}\,\mathrm{FF}/P_{\mathrm{in}}, &\quad \mathrm{FF} &= 0.65,\; P_{\mathrm{in}} = 100~\mathrm{mW\,cm^{-2}},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $J_{\max}(E_g)$ is the short-circuit current density available from AM1.5G photons above $E_g$. It is tabulated at 19 gaps between $0.5$ and $3.0$~eV, spanning $65$ to $1.8$~mA\,cm$^{-2}$. Values are interpolated linearly and clamped outside the table. The HOPV15 experimental block supplies $E_{\mathrm{HOMO}}$ and $E_g$ as measured values, rather than computed orbital energies. These are the electrochemical HOMO level and, where available, the optical gap. Gap selection uses the optical gap first ($194$ molecules), then the electrochemical gap ($46$), then the LUMO--HOMO difference ($0$). Molecules lacking a HOMO level, any gap or PCE were dropped ($350 \to 240$). No SMILES failed to parse. We apply the PC$_{61}$BM acceptor level to every donor. The reported devices used PC$_{71}$BM ($123$ molecules), PC$_{61}$BM ($101$) or other acceptors ($16$). The clamp at $V_{\mathrm{OC}} = 0$ is never active, with an LF minimum of $0.42\%$. LF values range from $0.42$ to $14.43\%$, and HF values from $0.008$ to $10.2\%$.

\paragraph{Band-gap target and elastic-modulus pools.}
Both fidelities of the two band-gap pools are replaced by $|E_g - 1.4~\mathrm{eV}|$ before optimization, so that the $R^2$ of Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks} is computed on the transformed values. The elastic-modulus pools are used on the linear GPa scale, negated, after removing the compounds without a finite, positive modulus at either fidelity.

\paragraph{Cost ratios.}
Cost ratios for synthetic functions ($0.1$ / $0.5$), COFs ($0.065$), FreeSolv ($0.1$) and polarizability ($0.167$) follow the studies introducing these pools~\citep{sabanza2025best,gantzler2023multifidelity,fare2022multi}. The remaining ratios are assumptions of this work. HOPV15 uses $0.1$ by analogy with FreeSolv because its Scharber proxy is a closed-form estimate from measurements rather than a calculation. Matbench-gap uses $0.05$, giving $20$ LF queries per HF query under the round-robin schedule. A literal PBE-to-experiment ratio would be much smaller and give hundreds of LF queries per HF query. ExptGap-PBE and the three elastic-modulus pools use $0.05$ by the same convention. For the latter, an MLIP elastic calculation costs roughly $10^{-3}$ of a DFT calculation. Because cost is charged per query, $\rho$ serves as a schedule parameter rather than a measured cost.

The remaining subsections define the two synthetic functions and their scenarios. They follow the Branin and Park benchmark families used for multi-fidelity Bayesian optimization (MFBO) by Sabanza-Gil et al.~\citep{sabanza2025best}. Their implemented definitions are given below.

\subsection{Branin function}
The HF Branin function is
\begin{equation}
f^{(H)}(x_1, x_2) = \left(x_2 - b x_1^2 + c x_1 - 6\right)^2 + 10\left(1 - \frac{1}{8\pi}\right)\cos(x_1) + 10,
\end{equation}
with $b = 5.1/(4\pi^2)$, $c = 5/\pi$, domain $x_1 \in [-5, 10]$, $x_2 \in [0, 15]$, and
global minimum $f^* = 0.397887$. The LF approximation modifies only the
quadratic coefficient, $b_{\text{LF}} = b - 0.1(1-\alpha)$, so that the correlation
degrades as $\alpha$ decreases.

\subsection{Park function}
The HF Park function is
\begin{equation}
f^{(H)}(x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4) = \frac{\tilde{x}_1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{(x_2 + x_3^2)\tilde{x}_4}{\tilde{x}_1^2 + \epsilon}} + (\tilde{x}_1 + 3\tilde{x}_4)\exp\!\left(1 + \sin(x_3)\right),
\end{equation}
with $x_i \in [0,1]$, $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$ and $\tilde{x}_j = \max(x_j,\epsilon)$ for $j=1,4$. The unregularized function has global minimum $f^* = 0$, approached as $x_1, x_4 \to 0$. This definition generates the candidate lattice and is implemented in the released code. Its first term differs from the classical Park function, $\tfrac{x_1}{2}\bigl[\sqrt{1 + (x_2 + x_3^2)x_4/x_1^2} - 1\bigr] + (x_1 + 3x_4)\exp(1 + \sin x_3)$. The guards on $x_1$ and $x_4$ and denominator regularization use $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$. The LF approximation modifies the $x_4$ coefficient ($3 \rightarrow 3 - 1.5(1-\alpha)$). It also scales the sine term in the exponent by $\alpha$, giving $\exp(1 + \alpha\sin(x_3))$.

\subsection{Scenario configurations}
Supplementary Table~\ref{supptab:synthetic_scenarios} lists the $\alpha$ values and resulting characteristics. To compute $R^2$, we sampled 1,000 uniform points, evaluated both fidelities and fitted a linear regression from LF to HF. This equals the squared LF--HF Pearson correlation defined in Methods. Candidate pools use uniform grids of $50^2 = 2{,}500$ points for Branin and $4^4 = 256$ for Park. The lattices include domain boundaries, and regret is measured relative to their best candidate rather than the analytical minimum. The best Branin lattice point has $f^{(H)} = 0.4045$, compared with the analytical $f^* = 0.3979$. The best Park lattice value is $1.1 \times 10^{-7}$.

\suppnote{Acquisition variants and calibration}{suppnote:acquisition_variants}

This section specifies the protocols for the matched-acquisition and acquisition-portfolio comparisons summarized in the main text. Supplementary Figs.~\ref{suppfig:acquisition} and~\ref{suppfig:portfolio} show the results. Aggregated result files are included in the code release.

\paragraph{Acquisition variants.} We evaluated five uncertainty-driven acquisitions in addition to the primary greedy policy. These span the standard taxonomy of myopic acquisition functions~\citep{shahriari2015taking}. Improvement-based policies were EI~\citep{jones1998efficient} and probability of improvement~\citep{kushner1964new}. The optimistic policy was the Gaussian-process lower confidence bound~\citep{srinivas2012information}, with $\beta=2$. Information-theoretic max-value entropy search~\citep{wang2017max} used a Gumbel approximation and ten max-value samples. Thompson sampling~\citep{russo2018tutorial} provided the sampling-based policy. Each replaced only the LF acquisition, leaving the surrogate, round-robin schedule, HF step and seeds fixed. Acquisition used the HF predictive mean, BLR standard deviation and HF incumbent (Supplementary Note~\ref{suppnote:blr}). All five transfer-learning surrogates ran on nine benchmarks with $5\times 9\times 20$ seeds per acquisition, using seeds 42--61.

For each acquisition and benchmark, we report the fraction of surrogates beating the greedy policy and the mean paired attainment difference. No acquisition raises mean attainment by more than about $0.07$. The largest gain is $+0.069$ for Thompson sampling on Branin-Unfav. Next are probability of improvement on HOPV15 ($+0.048$) and Branin-Fav ($+0.047$). Thompson sampling lowers attainment by $0.09$ on HOPV15 and $0.14$ on Matbench-gap. The lower confidence bound and max-value entropy search lower it by $0.03$--$0.11$ on the Branin functions (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{suppfig:portfolio}).

To isolate surrogate effects, we also ran GP-base with a greedy policy based on its posterior mean, holding other settings fixed (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{suppfig:acquisition}). Attainment stayed within $0.03$ of EI on eight of the nine benchmarks. On HOPV15, the greedy policy lowered attainment to $0.23$, compared with $0.32$ under EI. Conversely, TL-E2E with BLR-driven EI stayed within $0.01$ of its greedy attainment on seven benchmarks. It attained $0.04$ less on Branin-Fav and $0.07$ more on HOPV15 (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{suppfig:acquisition}a--i). Across the five transfer-learning surrogates, the sign of the EI effect on HOPV15 depends on the surrogate (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{suppfig:acquisition}j). On COFs, FreeSolv and polarizability, no portfolio acquisition improved mean attainment over the greedy policy by more than a small margin. When the transfer-learned mean already provides a useful ranking, exploration therefore has little to add.

\paragraph{Calibration.} For every surrogate, we computed LF expected calibration error (ECE), negative log-likelihood and sharpness on held-out candidates. We related these to attainment from Fig.~\ref{fig:final_regret}, with the resulting correlation shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}a--m. Across eight surrogates and thirteen benchmarks, the correlation between loop-mean held-out LF calibration error and attainment has no consistent sign. It exceeds $|r| = 0.5$ only on Matbench-gap and ExptGap-PBE ($r = -0.67$ and $-0.90$). There, the three GPs are both the worst-calibrated LF models and the weakest optimizers. Their calibration errors span $0.15$--$0.36$, compared with $0.08$--$0.12$ for transfer-learning surrogates. On COFs, every surrogate attains $0.90$--$0.93$, so the small range limits interpretation of the correlation ($r = -0.48$).

Good calibration also does not identify the best optimizers. GP-base has the lowest LF calibration error on seven of thirteen benchmarks. Yet it ranks in the lower half of the eight surrogates by attainment on seven of nine chemistry and materials pools. GP-DKL is the worst-calibrated on eight benchmarks but among the best optimizers on the Branin functions. ECE is averaged over refits recorded in each source run, whose horizon can differ from the attainment budget. The held-out LF calibration error uses 34--40 seeds per cell. Of 104 cells, 11 have fewer than 40. These are GP-SV on Branin-Fav and Branin-Unfav, GP-base on FreeSolv, and GP-base and GP-DKL on the four large materials pools. For these large pools, not every run records calibration error. For TL models, calibration assesses the separate LF BLR head rather than the HF head driving acquisition.

\suppnote{Surrogate-free LF screen and screening regimes}{suppnote:screen}

The surrogate-free screen in Fig.~\ref{fig:topk}a,b ranks candidates by LF value and evaluates the top-ranked candidate at HF. Its screening regret is $f_H(\arg\min f_L) - \min f_H$ in the pool's objective units, with ties resolved by first index. This indicates whether the LF proxy alone identifies a good candidate, rather than serving as a budget-matched optimization baseline. We divide benchmarks at screening regret $0.3$, with Regime~A at or below this boundary and Regime~B above. This is not a common accuracy tolerance across tasks.

In Regime~A, represented by Park-Fav and polarizability, LF--HF top-1\% overlap is approximately $1$ and screening regret is at most $0.09$. The screen therefore already identifies a near-optimal candidate. In Regime~B, high global correlation can conceal poor agreement among top-ranked candidates. COFs has a Spearman rank correlation of $0.998$ but screening regret $4.52$. Although both COFs and polarizability have $R^2 \approx 0.96$ or higher, their screening outcomes differ. HOPV15 and Matbench-gap fall in Regime~B, with screening regrets of $3.90$ and $0.91$.

The four larger materials pools also fall in Regime~B. On ExptGap-PBE, the screen misses the optimum by $1.15$~eV. The first indexed HF optimum sits at LF rank $1{,}033$ of $3{,}508$. On the elastic-modulus pools, screening regrets are $384$~GPa for CHGNet and $369$~GPa for SevenNet, or $74\%$ and $97\%$ of their HF ranges. MatterSim has screening regret $3$~GPa, or $0.6\%$ of the range, with the HF optimum at LF rank 2. These related pools therefore span nearly sufficient to uninformative screening. The best transfer-learning surrogates reach similarly low regret where screening is near-optimal and improve on it where LF rankings are less useful. On COFs, every surrogate reaches zero regret within the budget (Fig.~\ref{fig:regret_trajectory}e).

\suppnote{Computational cost: absolute FLOPs and compute ranking}{suppnote:computational_cost}

Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling_law} shows how surrogate compute scales with problem size. Here, we report the absolute costs underlying those laws. Each profile takes the median over seeds 42--43 at training-set fractions $0.1$, $0.3$, $0.5$, $0.7$ and $1.0$. Total loop floating-point operations (FLOPs) include one surrogate fit and one pool-wide prediction per iteration. Costs are interpolated from five profiled training-set sizes and summed over the loop. We profile the surrogates of Figs.~\ref{fig:final_regret} and~\ref{fig:regret_trajectory}, including the BLR head. GP-SV and GP-DKL cost up to $22\times$ and $15\times$ the cheapest transfer-learning surrogate, respectively, and GP-base up to $5.6\times$. GP-base is the cheapest surrogate on Park-Unfav, polarizability and HOPV15. TL-PtJ is the cheapest transfer-learning surrogate on every benchmark. Average compute ranks across the nine benchmarks, where lower is cheaper, are $1.3$ for TL-PtJ, $2.3$ for TL-SPS and $3.3$ for TL-MMD. TL-base and GP-base both rank $4.3$, followed by TL-E2E at $5.3$, GP-DKL at $7.0$ and GP-SV at $8.0$. FLOPs are accumulated over the budgets in Fig.~\ref{fig:final_regret}, except FreeSolv at 50 and Matbench-gap at 20.
